Valence electrons & Lewis dot symbols

For main-group elements, the number of valence electrons (outer-shell s + p electrons) equals the last digit of the group number (groups 13–18) or the group number itself (groups 1–2). Helium is the one oddball: group 18 but only 2. Those are the only electrons drawn in Lewis structures.

The step-by-step method

This recipe works for almost every molecule and polyatomic ion in general chemistry.

Step 1

Count valence electrons

Add up valence electrons of all atoms. Add one per negative charge, subtract one per positive charge.

Step 2

Pick the central atom

Usually the least electronegative atom (often the one there's only one of). H and F are never central. H forms only one bond.

Step 3

Draw single bonds

Connect each outer atom to the central atom. Each bond uses 2 electrons; subtract them.

Step 4

Complete outer octets

Give outer atoms lone pairs until each has 8 electrons (H: 2).

Step 5

Leftovers on the center

Place any remaining electrons on the central atom (period 3+ atoms may exceed 8).

Step 6

Multiple bonds & FC

If the central atom lacks an octet, convert outer lone pairs into double/triple bonds. Check formal charges.

Formal charge: choosing the best structure

Formal charge pretends every bond is shared perfectly equally and asks: does this atom "own" more or fewer electrons than it brought?

FC = V − L − B2
V = valence electrons of the free atom · L = nonbonding (lone-pair) electrons · B = bonding electrons around the atom (so B/2 = number of bonds)

Resonance

When one Lewis structure can't capture the bonding, we draw several resonance forms that differ only in where electrons sit (atoms never move). The real molecule is a single hybrid, an average of the forms, not a molecule flipping back and forth.

Resonance hybrid (what's really there)

Reading curved arrows

A curved arrow shows an electron pair moving: tail = where the pair starts (lone pair or bond), head = where it ends up. Going from one resonance form to the next, a lone pair becomes a new π bond, and an old π bond becomes a lone pair.

Bond order in the hybrid

Average bond order = total bonds between equivalent positions ÷ number of positions. NO₃⁻: 4 bonds over 3 N–O links = 4/3. O₃: 3 bonds over 2 links = 1.5. More bonds means shorter and stronger: O₃'s two O–O bonds are identical (128 pm), between O–O (148 pm) and O=O (121 pm).

Exceptions to the octet rule

1. Incomplete octets

Be and B (and Al in some gas-phase compounds) can be stable with fewer than 8 electrons. These electron-deficient molecules are Lewis acids: BF₃ readily accepts a lone pair from NH₃.

2. Odd-electron species

An odd electron total means at least one unpaired electron: a free radical. Radicals are usually very reactive. Put the odd electron where formal charges are best (often on the less electronegative atom).

3. Expanded octets

Atoms from period 3 and below (P, S, Cl, Br, I, Xe…) can be surrounded by more than 8 electrons. They're larger, so more atoms fit around them. Period-2 atoms (C, N, O, F) never exceed 8.

About "d orbitals": older texts say expanded octets use empty 3d orbitals. Modern calculations show d orbitals contribute very little. Hypervalent bonding is better described by delocalized (3-center, 4-electron) bonds plus ionic character. For Lewis structures and exams, the rule "period 3+ can exceed an octet" is what matters.

From Lewis structure to 3D shape (VSEPR)

Count electron domains on the central atom: each lone pair, single bond, double bond, or triple bond counts as one domain. Domains spread out as far as possible (electron geometry); the molecular shape describes only where the atoms are.

Polarity in two questions: (1) Are there polar bonds (electronegativity difference)? (2) Do the bond dipoles cancel by symmetry? Symmetric shapes with identical outer atoms (linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral, trigonal bipyramidal, octahedral, square planar) are nonpolar. Lone pairs on the central atom usually make the shape asymmetric → polar (exceptions: XeF₂, XeF₄, I₃⁻).

Drawing a Lewis structure

1. Total valence e⁻ (−charge adds, +charge subtracts)
2. Central atom = least electronegative (never H or F)
3. Single bonds to every outer atom (2 e⁻ each)
4. Complete outer octets (H: 2)
5. Leftovers → central atom
6. Central short? Lone pair → multiple bond
7. Check formal charges; brackets + charge for ions

Formal charge

FC = V − L − B/2   (= valence − dots − bonds)
Best: FCs near 0; − on most electronegative atom; ΣFC = overall charge

Valence electrons by group

Group 1: 1 · 2: 2 · 13: 3 · 14: 4 · 15: 5 · 16: 6 · 17: 7 · 18: 8 (He: 2)

Typical neutral bonding

H, F, Cl: 1 bond + 0/3 lone pairs · O: 2 bonds + 2 LP · N: 3 bonds + 1 LP · C: 4 bonds · B: 3 bonds (6 e⁻)

Octet exceptions

Incomplete: B, Be (BF₃, BeCl₂) · Odd e⁻ (radicals): NO, NO₂ · Expanded (period 3+ only): PCl₅, SF₆, XeF₄, I₃⁻

Resonance

Same atoms, different electron placement · real molecule = hybrid · bond order = bonds ÷ positions (NO₃⁻, CO₃²⁻: 4/3; O₃, SO₂, C₆H₆: 1.5)

VSEPR (domains on central atom; multiple bond = 1 domain)

Polarity

Polar bonds + asymmetric shape → polar molecule. Symmetric shapes with identical outer atoms → nonpolar (CO₂, BF₃, CH₄, PCl₅, SF₆, XeF₄). Usually polar: H₂O, NH₃, SO₂, SF₄, ClF₃, BrF₅.

Electronegativity (Pauling)

F 3.98 > O 3.44 > Cl 3.16 > N 3.04 > Br 2.96 > I 2.66 > S 2.58 ≈ C 2.55 > H 2.20 ≈ P 2.19 > B 2.04

Must-know structures

CO₂ O=C=O · HCN H–C≡N · N₂ N≡N · CO₃²⁻ / NO₃⁻: 3 resonance forms · O₃, SO₂: 2 forms · NH₄⁺: N is +1 · OH⁻: O is −1 · CN⁻: C is −1
