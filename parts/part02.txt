<!-- 7 EXCEPTIONS -->
<section id="exceptions" data-title="Exceptions">
  <div class="wrap">
    <div class="sec-head"><div class="sec-num">7</div><h2>Exceptions to the octet rule</h2></div>
    <div class="grid3">
      <div class="card"><h3>1. Incomplete octets</h3><p class="small">Be and B (and Al in some gas-phase compounds) can be stable with fewer than 8 electrons. These <b>electron-deficient</b> molecules are Lewis acids: BF₃ readily accepts a lone pair from NH₃.</p></div>
      <div class="card"><h3>2. Odd-electron species</h3><p class="small">An odd electron total means at least one unpaired electron: a <b>free radical</b>. Radicals are usually very reactive. Put the odd electron where formal charges are best (often on the less electronegative atom).</p></div>
      <div class="card"><h3>3. Expanded octets</h3><p class="small">Atoms from <b>period 3 and below</b> (P, S, Cl, Br, I, Xe…) can be surrounded by more than 8 electrons. They're larger, so more atoms fit around them. Period-2 atoms (C, N, O, F) <b>never</b> exceed 8.</p></div>
    </div>
    <div class="molgrid" id="excGrid"></div>
    <div class="tip warn"><b>About "d orbitals":</b> older texts say expanded octets use empty 3d orbitals. Modern calculations show d orbitals contribute very little. Hypervalent bonding is better described by delocalized (3-center, 4-electron) bonds plus ionic character. For Lewis structures and exams, the rule "period 3+ can exceed an octet" is what matters.</div>
  </div>
</section>
